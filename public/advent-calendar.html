Advent Calendar Season: What Drops, When, and Where to Buy

Advent calendars no longer appear in December — the ones people actually want usually open for orders in autumn, and the best-known runs are gone before most shoppers start looking. What you need is the release window for each type, a rough sense of what each category costs, and a way to tell a limited brand run from a supermarket box. This page covers those three things.

When Advent Calendars Actually Go on Sale

Advent calendars rarely appear all at once. Beauty and skincare calendars are typically announced in late summer and sell through early autumn, well before the holiday season begins. Chocolate and grocery calendars tend to reach stores closer to November. Toy and building-set calendars fall somewhere in between, often timed to a retailer's own holiday reset rather than a fixed date.

The practical result is that the calendar you want may be sold out before the ones you would expect are even listed. If you have a specific brand in mind, the date that matters is not December 1 — it is the day that brand opens orders. A newsletter sign-up or a brand's own social account usually gives a few days' notice, which is often the difference between buying at list price and paying a reseller.

Release dates also shift year to year and differ by country. A set launched in September in one market may appear in November in another, or not be sold there at all. Treat any single date as approximate until the brand confirms it on its own channels.

Which Category Fits Your Budget

Chocolate and candy calendars are the cheapest and the most widely stocked; supermarkets and drugstores generally carry several side by side. They are also the type most likely to be reduced in late November, because stock is plentiful and retailers want the shelf space back once December arrives.

Beauty and grooming calendars cost considerably more, and whether they are good value depends on whether you would use the contents. A set built from sample sizes can look generous on paper while delivering items too small to repurchase. Read the full contents list before comparing headline prices between two boxes.

Toy, building-set and collectible calendars sit in the middle. Their appeal is usually one exclusive item — a specific figure, a piece you cannot buy separately — rather than the daily reveal itself. On those, the contents list matters far more than the size of the box or the number of doors.

Brand Calendars and Limited Runs

Fashion, beauty and lifestyle brands increasingly release their own calendars as limited runs. These usually go on sale through the brand's own site rather than general retailers, sell in a single drop, and are rarely produced again once the run is gone. That makes them closer to a product launch than to seasonal grocery stock.

Searches for a specific brand's calendar — Skims is a common one — usually come down to three questions: whether it exists this year, when it opens, and where it can be bought. The honest answer is that it varies by season. A brand that ran a calendar one year may skip the next, so the brand's own announcements are the only dependable confirmation.

Door Counts and What's Inside

Most calendars run 24 doors and finish on Christmas Eve, but 12-door versions are common at lower price points and suit anyone who wants a shorter run. A smaller number does not automatically mean less value — it often means larger individual items, or simply a cheaper format.

Packaging is part of the product. A reusable box, a drawer-style case or a hard-backed book usually accounts for part of the price and tends to be kept after December, which is why those formats cost more than a plain card sleeve. If you are comparing two sets with similar contents, the format is often the real difference.

Sell-Outs, Restocks and Last-Minute Buying

Popular calendars can sell out months ahead of December. When that happens, a restock is the exception rather than the rule: a limited run is usually produced once, and a second batch depends on whether the brand is holding unsold stock. A sold-out listing is not a promise that more are coming.

Waiting for a discount is a gamble that pays off unevenly. Chocolate calendars are frequently reduced after mid-November, because retailers would rather clear them than store them. Limited beauty and brand calendars rarely are, and waiting usually means losing the item rather than saving on it.

If a calendar sells out before you get to it, check whether the brand runs a waitlist or a back-in-stock notification. Be cautious with resale: prices in December are often well above the original, and a sealed box bought secondhand carries no guarantee about the contents inside.

FAQ

When do advent calendars go on sale?

Usually from late summer through November, depending on the category. Beauty calendars generally open first, while chocolate and grocery calendars tend to reach stores closer to November.

Does Skims have an advent calendar?

It depends on the year, and availability is not guaranteed. Any release would be announced on Skims' own site and channels, so check there rather than relying on reseller listings.

How much do advent calendars cost?

Prices vary widely. Supermarket chocolate calendars are the cheapest, beauty and brand calendars cost much more, and premium or reusable-format sets sit at the top. Contents and packaging drive the price more than door count.

Do advent calendars sell out?

Limited brand and beauty runs often do, sometimes weeks before December. Mass-market chocolate calendars rarely sell out and are frequently discounted in late November.

What is the difference between 12 and 24 doors?

A 24-door calendar runs up to Christmas Eve, while a 12-door version is usually a shorter or cheaper format. Door count alone does not indicate value — check the contents list.
